[Stanford CS329A #6] Train-Time Scaling & RL: DeepSeekMath, GRPO, and Large-Scale Reasoning Optimization

November 6, 2026 · 14 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the sixth installment of our definitive engineering breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

In Lectures 1 through 5 (Part 1: Foundation), we examined how agents push the limits of frozen, pre-trained models by allocating test-time compute: exploring search trees with MCTS, gathering runtime compiler diagnostics, and pruning dead ends.

Yet, we must confront a sober mathematical reality: If an elementary school student has never been taught the principles of calculus, giving them ten additional minutes to think during an exam will not produce a valid integration proof. Similarly, if an autoregressive model lacks the foundational representations within its frozen synaptic weights, sampling 10,000 rollouts or executing greedy beam search does not yield genuine intelligence—it produces stochastic luck.

Today marks the launch of Part 2: Training & Evolution (Lectures 6–9). We pivot from inference heuristics to fundamental neural weight updates:

"Instead of burning inference compute at runtime, how can we invest massive compute during training time to permanently rewrite the neural synapses of the model into an elite reasoner?"

In Lecture 6, we dismantle the core mathematical engine driving frontier reasoning models: Post-Training Reinforcement Learning (RL). We analyze three foundational paradigms that eliminated human labeling bottlenecks and scaled mathematical reasoning:

  1. Autonomous Reasoning Bootstrapping: Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR, Zelikman et al., Stanford 2022).
  2. The Death of the Critic: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO, DeepSeekMath 2024).
  3. Scaling to 1,000 GPUs: Decoupled Clipping and Dynamic Sampling (DAPO, 2025).

[Stanford CS329A #5] Multi-Step Planning & Tree Search: ADaPT, LATS, SPRINT, and Language Agent MCTS

November 5, 2026 · 14 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the fifth installment of our deep-dive engineering breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

In Lecture 4, we witnessed how agents step outside their closed rooms to interact with external tools and debug code via compiler feedback loops. However, fixing local syntax bugs or querying an API is a far cry from architecting a complex 100,000-line distributed software system or resolving long-horizon scientific problems.

Standard ReAct and Chain-of-Thought approaches are fundamentally greedy, single-path searchers: they inspect the immediate next step and march forward irreversibly. Like an amateur chess player who only evaluates the next move and falls into a checkmate three turns later, a greedy language model that commits to a suboptimal early derivation becomes hopelessly trapped in a dead end, unable to recover.

Today's Lecture 5 serves as the grand finale of Part 1 (Foundation) of the Stanford curriculum: Multi-Step Planning and Tree Search.

By transplanting the mathematical engine of DeepMind's AlphaGo—Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)—into autoregressive language models, we transform reactive text predictors into strategic chess grandmasters. We explore systems that anticipate consequences dozens of steps ahead, dynamically prune dead ends, backtrack across history branches, and parallelize reasoning pipelines:

  1. Unifying Reasoning, Acting, and Planning: Language Agent Tree Search (LATS, Zhou et al., 2023).
  2. As-Needed Recursive Decomposition: Breaking down complex objectives only upon failure via ADaPT (Allen AI, 2023).
  3. Parallel Dependency DAG Packing: Slashing multi-step inference latency by 53.5% via SPRINT (Stanford Future Data, 2025).
  4. Adaptive Branching via Thompson Sampling: Solving the Wider vs. Deeper dilemma with AB-MCTS (Sakana AI, 2025).
  5. Internalizing Planning into Synaptic Weights: SWiRL (Step-Wise RL, 2025).

[Stanford CS329A #4] Tool Use & Code Feedback: ReAct, RLEF, and Constitutional Self-Correction

November 4, 2026 · 14 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the fourth installment of our engineering breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

In Lectures 2 and 3, we explored how test-time compute scaling and Process Reward Models (PRMs) allow models to explore reasoning trees and verify intermediate mathematical derivations. Yet, despite their sophisticated internal reasoning chains, models constrained to pure text generation remain "closed-room test takers"—entirely dependent on static, frozen parametric memory acquired during pre-training. When faced with evolving external facts, dynamic APIs, or syntax-critical code execution, an ungrounded model inevitably hallucinates, incapable of verifying its outputs against real-world systems.

Today, we break out of that closed room. Lecture 4 marks the structural pivot from passive language models to active, closed-loop autonomous agents: Tool Use and Execution Feedback Loops.

Instead of treating code or API calls as final text generation targets, modern autonomous agents treat tools as interactive sensors and actuators. In this lecture, we dissect the three fundamental tiers of agentic feedback:

  1. Prompt-Level Environment Feedback: Unifying reasoning and actions via ReAct (Yao et al., 2022).
  2. Policy-Level Execution Feedback: Baking compiler and test-runner diagnostics directly into neural weights via RLEF (Reinforcement Learning from Execution Feedback, 2024).
  3. Self-Supervised Value Alignment: Replacing expensive human evaluators with autonomous critique-revision loops via Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022).

[Stanford CS329A #3] Robust Verification: Process Reward Models (PRM), Ensembles, and Scalable Oversight

November 3, 2026 · 13 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the third installment of our definitive engineering breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

In Lecture 2, we examined the mechanics of test-time compute scaling and reached a sobering conclusion: while generating thousands of candidate trajectories ($pass@k$) pushes theoretical solution coverage above 99%, this raw potential remains fundamentally unusable without a high-precision verifier. When evaluated with crude heuristics like majority voting, model accuracy hits an insurmountable plateau.

Today, we confront the core architectural engine that breaks through this bottleneck: Robust Verification.

Human mathematicians and software engineers rarely solve complex problems in an unthinking, single-shot stream of consciousness; they write down a step, verify its algebraic consistency, catch subtle arithmetic slips, and backtrack before committing to subsequent derivations. Conversely, standard autoregressive language models operate strictly forward in time. Once an early token wanders off course, the model hallucinates increasingly convoluted rationalizations to justify its initial error.

How do we design independent verifier models capable of inspecting intermediate reasoning steps, diagnosing logical fallacies, and guiding autonomous agents to ground-truth solutions without requiring millions of dollars in human annotation?

Through the foundational research of Cobbe et al. (GSM8K, OpenAI, 2021), Lightman et al. (PRM800K, 2023), automated Monte Carlo step annotation (Math-Shepherd, Yu et al., 2023), and the state-of-the-art Weaver ensemble framework (NeurIPS 2025), we will dismantle and rebuild the state of the art in Verifier Engineering.

[Stanford CS329A #2] Test-Time Compute Scaling: Beyond Train-Time Scaling Laws to Compute-Optimal Inference

November 2, 2026 · 12 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the second installment of our definitive engineering breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

In our inaugural lecture, we traced the macroeconomic paradigm shift from static, autoregressive next-token predictors to autonomous self-improving agentic loops. Today, we confront the technical foundation that makes this autonomy computationally viable: Test-Time Compute Scaling (Inference-Time Scaling).

For over half a decade, the predominant scaling doctrine across the AI industry was dictated by train-time laws (Kaplan et al., Chinchilla): allocate larger clusters, expand parameter footprints, and absorb tens of trillions of tokens. Yet, once a model’s parameters θ are frozen, what happens if we refuse to accept a 50-millisecond greedy response? What if an agent spends 10 seconds, 1 minute, or 10 minutes deliberately exploring reasoning trees, checking intermediate steps, and revising its hypotheses before committing to an action?

Through the foundational empirical discoveries of Large Language Monkeys (Brown et al., 2024), Snell et al.'s Compute-Optimal Scaling (Stanford/UC Berkeley, 2024), and the Archon modular architecture framework, we will mathematically formalize how trading test-time FLOPs for parameter capacity reshapes modern artificial intelligence.

[Stanford CS329A #1] Course Overview: From Large Language Models to Self-Improving AI Agents

November 1, 2026 · 12 min read
Jaybee
AI & Robotics Engineer

Welcome to the definitive technical breakdown of Stanford University's CS329A: Self-Improving AI Agents (Autumn 2025).

Over the past four years, the artificial intelligence landscape has been dominated by a single, powerful dogma: pre-training larger models on larger text corpora yields ever-increasing cognitive capabilities. While this paradigm sparked the modern generative AI revolution—giving rise to GPT-4, Claude 3.5, and Gemini—the frontier of machine learning research has hit a pivotal inflection point.

Static foundation models, however immense their parameter counts, remain fundamentally bounded: they are passive, autoregressive next-token predictors operating in an open-loop regime. Once their weights are frozen post-training, they cannot dynamically allocate compute to harder problems, cannot autonomously verify the factual correctness of their solutions, and cannot iteratively self-correct their errors based on environmental feedback.

        D2 -->|Reward & Error Signal| B2
        D2 ==>|Test-Time & Train-Time Update| E2[Continuous Self-Evolution]

Stanford CS329A addresses the central question of the next decade of AI: How do we build software agents that do not merely generate text, but continuously inspect, verify, plan, and improve their own reasoning and code?

In this inaugural overview lecture, we deconstruct the evolutionary trajectory from foundational LLMs to agentic systems, formalize the mathematics of scaling laws and inference-time compute allocation, analyze the four canonical agent workflow archetypes, and isolate the critical bottleneck of autonomous intelligence: the Generator-Verifier Gap.
